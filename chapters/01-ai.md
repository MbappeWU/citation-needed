<a id="ai-code-quality"></a>
### AI tools do not hurt code quality.

**✗ Not supported · Grade C** (The only causal estimate cited here covers a single tool in open-source projects and uses proxy measures. The rest is a vendor report and self-reported survey data.)

**What the research says.** A Carnegie Mellon difference-in-differences study compared 807 GitHub projects that adopted Cursor (January 2024 to March 2025) with 1,380 matched controls. Lines added jumped in the first month and faded within about two months. Static-analysis warnings rose about 30% and code complexity about 40%, and both stayed high. GitClear's vendor analysis of 211 million changed lines (2020 to 2024) reports more copy-pasted code and less moved (refactored) code. DORA 2024 respondents, by contrast, tied 25% more AI adoption to 3.4% higher self-reported code quality.

**Limits.** Warnings and complexity are proxies, not production defects. The CMU study covers open-source projects and one tool (2024 to early 2025). GitClear measures trends in git history and cannot show cause. DORA's quality figure is self-reported.

> **Say this to your boss:** A Carnegie Mellon study of 807 projects found static-analysis warnings up about 30% and complexity up about 40% after Cursor adoption, while the speed gain faded within two months. Let's keep review and lint gates and track rework.

**What would change my mind.** Matched or randomized studies of current agents showing no rise in defects, warnings or rework after adoption, ideally in company repositories.

<a id="ai-code-security"></a>
### AI-generated code is as secure as human-written code.

**✗ Not supported · Grade C** (Benchmarks show frequent vulnerabilities, but none compares against human code at scale, and the two user studies disagree.)

**What the research says.** In a 2021 test, about 40% of 1,689 programs from GitHub Copilot across 89 scenarios were vulnerable. In a Stanford user study, participants with an AI assistant (codex-davinci-002) wrote significantly less secure code and were more likely to think it was secure. A 58-person C study found AI-assisted users made critical security bugs at a rate no more than 10% higher than controls. Veracode's 2025 vendor benchmark (100+ models, 80 tasks) found 45% of outputs had a known vulnerability; its 2026 updates report about 55 to 56% secure.

**Limits.** None of these compares AI and human code at scale in production, and humans also write vulnerable code. Tools range from 2021 Copilot to 2025 models, and results vary by language and prompt. Veracode is a vendor report.

> **Say this to your boss:** The evidence is thin. Tests find vulnerable output in about 40 to 45% of samples, one user study found AI users wrote less secure code while feeling more sure of it, and another found little difference. Let's keep security review and scanning on AI changes.

**What would change my mind.** A large comparison of AI-assisted and human-only code, using current tools and independent vulnerability testing, that finds no difference in flaw rates.

<a id="ai-delivery"></a>
### Adopting AI improves software delivery performance.

**~ It depends · Grade C** (DORA's surveys are large but self-reported and correlational, and the throughput result changed sign between 2024 and 2025.)

**What the research says.** DORA's 2024 survey estimated that a 25% rise in AI adoption went with 1.5% lower delivery throughput and 7.2% lower delivery stability, but also 3.4% higher code quality and 3.1% faster code review. DORA 2025 (nearly 5,000 respondents, 90% using AI) reported a positive link between AI adoption and throughput, and still a negative link with stability. A 2026 NBER working paper (100,000+ GitHub developers) found AI raised coding activity far more than releases.

**Limits.** DORA data are self-reported survey answers about AI use in general, not one tool generation, so they show association, not cause. The 2024 effects are estimates for a 25% rise in adoption. DORA is run by Google Cloud, which sells AI tools. The NBER paper is a working paper, not peer reviewed.

> **Say this to your boss:** DORA found AI adoption linked to lower stability in both years and to higher throughput only in 2025. Faster coding is not faster delivery, so let's watch change failure rate and lead time, not code volume.

**What would change my mind.** Controlled or longitudinal data from real organizations showing that AI adoption raises throughput while change failure rate stays flat or falls.

**Sources** (checked 2026-10-01):

1. DORA, Google Cloud (2024). Accelerate State of DevOps Report 2024. Google Cloud DORA. [link](https://research.google/pubs/dora-accelerate-state-of-devops-2024-report/) <sub>↗ not machine-checkable (book or report); link only</sub> (primary)
2. DORA, Google Cloud (2025). 2025 DORA Report: State of AI-assisted Software Development. DORA / Google Cloud. [link](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/announcing-the-2025-dora-report) <sub>↗ not machine-checkable (book or report); link only</sub> (primary)

<a id="ai-experienced-faster"></a>
### AI tools make experienced developers faster.

**~ It depends · Grade C** (The best randomized trial is small (16 developers) and used early-2025 tools. Its follow-up points to speedups, but its authors call the data unreliable.)

**What the research says.** In METR's randomized trial (early 2025), 16 experienced open-source developers did 246 tasks in their own mature repositories, using Cursor Pro with Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet. Tasks took 19% longer with AI. A second study from August 2025 (newer tools, 800+ tasks) estimated 18% less time for its 10 returning developers and 4% less for 47 new ones; both intervals include zero. METR says speedups now seem likely but calls the data unreliable, because more developers refused to work without AI. A Science study of about 160,000 GitHub developers (observational) found the gains went to experienced developers.

**Limits.** METR's setting is narrow: maintainers fixing issues in code they know well. Unfamiliar code, new features and newer agents may give different results. The later estimates come from a self-selected sample, so they cannot settle the question.

> **Say this to your boss:** A randomized trial of experienced developers in their own code found early-2025 tools slowed them by 19%. Newer data hint at a speedup, but the researchers call it unreliable. Let's time a few real tasks ourselves.

**What would change my mind.** A new randomized trial without the selection problem, using current agents on experienced developers' own repositories, with a confidence interval that excludes zero.

<a id="ai-faster"></a>
### AI coding tools make developers faster.

**✓ Supported · Grade B** (Three randomized studies agree on a gain, but sizes range from 21% to 55.8%, they tested earlier tools, and one 2025 trial found a slowdown.)

**What the research says.** In a 2023 preprint, 95 developers wrote an HTTP server in JavaScript, and the GitHub Copilot group finished 55.8% faster. At Google, 96 engineers did one enterprise task and AI cut time by about 21%, with a wide confidence interval. Three field experiments at Microsoft, Accenture and a Fortune 100 firm (4,867 developers) found 26.08% more completed tasks. The 55.8% figure is the high end. Estimates from company settings are lower.

**Limits.** Studies differ in tool, task and metric, and intervals are wide (standard error 10.3% in the field experiments). The three studies predate today's coding agents. Two other trials found no gain: experienced maintainers in their own repositories (19% slower, 2025) and 52 engineers learning a new library (no significant speedup, 2026).

> **Say this to your boss:** Randomized studies find real but modest gains: about 21% less time at Google and 26% more tasks in company trials. The 55% figure comes from one small lab task. Let's plan for a modest gain and measure our own.

**What would change my mind.** Repeated randomized trials in real company work that show no gain, or a slowdown, for typical developers using current tools.

<a id="ai-juniors-more"></a>
### AI helps junior developers more than seniors.

**~ It depends · Grade C** (Randomized field experiments point one way and a large observational study points the other, with different outcomes and tools.)

**What the research says.** Three field experiments at Microsoft, Accenture and a Fortune 100 firm (4,867 developers, GitHub Copilot) found 26.08% more completed tasks, with higher adoption and larger gains among less experienced developers. A customer-support study (not software) also found novices gained most. A Science study of about 160,000 GitHub developers (Python, through 2024) found the reverse: early-career developers used AI most but showed no measurable gain, while experienced developers captured the gains. METR's 2025 trial studied only experienced developers.

**Limits.** The two sources measure experience and output differently: the field experiments count completed tasks, while the Science study infers AI use with a classifier on public GitHub code and counts commit activity. Neither measures code quality or learning.

> **Say this to your boss:** The studies disagree on who gains more. Controlled experiments inside companies favored less experienced developers; a large GitHub analysis favored seniors. I would not plan headcount on either claim without our own data.

**What would change my mind.** A randomized trial that gives AI to both juniors and seniors on the same real tasks and measures completed work and quality.

The Effects of Generative AI on High-Skilled Work: Evidence from Three Field Experiments with Software Developers. Management Science. [link](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2025.00535) <sub>✓ metadata checked (title, authors, year)</sub> (primary)

<a id="ai-learning"></a>
### Using AI does not hurt how well developers learn.

**✗ Not supported · Grade B** (A randomized trial with developers and a larger one in school math agree, but the developer trial is small and short.)

**What the research says.** Anthropic randomized 52 mostly junior engineers learning the Python library Trio. With a sidebar AI assistant they scored 50% on a later quiz versus 67% without (17 points lower, p = 0.01), with the biggest gap on debugging. They finished only about two minutes faster, not significantly. Those who asked conceptual questions kept more. In a PNAS trial of nearly 1,000 high school students, unrestricted GPT-4 access raised practice scores but hurt later exam results; a guardrailed tutor version largely avoided this.

**Limits.** One library, a short session, a chat assistant rather than an agent, and a small sample. Harm is not automatic: a study of programming students found no overall effect, and using AI to explain beat using it to write.

> **Say this to your boss:** In a randomized trial with developers, people learning a new library with an AI assistant scored 17 points lower on a quiz and did not finish significantly faster. For new hires and new tools, let's use AI to explain, not to write it for them.

**What would change my mind.** Randomized trials of current coding agents showing equal or better skill retention on delayed tests in realistic onboarding work.

**Sources** (checked 2026-10-01):

1. Judy Hanwen Shen, Alex Tamkin (2026). How AI Impacts Skill Formation. Anthropic (arXiv preprint). [link](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.20245) <sub>✓ metadata checked (title, authors, year)</sub> (primary)
2. Hamsa Bastani, Osbert Bastani, Alp Sungu, Haosen Ge, Özge Kabakcı, Rei Mariman (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 122(26). [link](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122) <sub>✓ metadata checked (title, authors, year)</sub> (context)

<a id="ai-old-studies"></a>
### Those AI studies are out of date, because today's tools are much better.

**~ It depends · Grade C** (The only rerun cited here (METR) is flagged unreliable by its authors. The other newer sources are a working paper and vendor reports.)

**What the research says.** METR now calls its 2025 finding (19% slower with Cursor and Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet) outdated and says speedups seem likely, but its newer data are unreliable because more developers refused to work without AI. A 2026 NBER working paper (100,000+ GitHub developers) found each tool generation (autocomplete, interactive agents, autonomous agents) raised coding activity more, but far less at the level of releases. Veracode's 2026 reports still show about 55 to 56% secure outputs, with no gain from larger models. The learning trial used a sidebar chat assistant, not an agent.

**Limits.** Newer tools may do better or worse, and no retest with agents is cited here for learning or quality. METR's later estimate and the NBER paper are not peer reviewed, and Veracode is a vendor report. Tie every number to the tool generation it names.

> **Say this to your boss:** The 2025 results are dated, and METR says so itself. But the newer evidence is thin and mixed, so let's treat both the old slowdown and the new speedup as unproven and test current tools on our own work.

**What would change my mind.** Peer-reviewed randomized trials of current coding agents in company settings that report speed, quality, security and learning outcomes.

<a id="ai-self-estimate"></a>
### Developers can tell whether AI is speeding them up.

**✗ Not supported · Grade C** (One randomized trial measured both belief and actual time, with only 16 developers, and its result needs more studies to confirm.)

**What the research says.** In METR's 2025 randomized trial, 16 experienced developers expected AI to cut task time by 24% before they started. Tasks done with AI actually took 19% longer. Afterwards the developers still estimated that AI had cut their time by 20%. Belief and measurement pointed in opposite directions. In DORA 2025, over 80% of nearly 5,000 respondents said AI had raised their productivity, but surveys like this record belief, not time.

**Limits.** Small sample: 16 experienced open-source maintainers working in their own repositories with early-2025 tools. Judgment may be better with newer tools. The trial shows people can be wrong, not that they always are.

> **Say this to your boss:** In a randomized trial that checked, developers believed AI saved them 20% of their time while it cost them 19%. Instead of going by feel, let's time a few tasks with and without AI.

**What would change my mind.** Several randomized trials in which developers' predicted and measured speedups agree closely, including with current tools.
